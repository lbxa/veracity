The paper includes a collusion-resistance argument for randomly selected odd-number juries. If a pool of $N$ jurors contains $k$ colluders, define $p = k/N$ and draw a panel of size $n = 2m + 1$. The panel reaches a verdict when at least $t = m + 1$ jurors agree. With $X \sim \mathrm{Hypergeometric}(N,k,n)$, Hoeffding's inequality gives

$$
P(X \ge t) \le \exp\left[-2n\left(\frac{1}{2} - p\right)^2\right].
$$

The bound is useful because the probability of a successful collusion attempt falls exponentially as panel size $n$ increases, provided the dishonest share remains below one half.

The numerical appendix turns that theorem into operating guidance. For a 10 percent corruption level, the exact hypergeometric tail probability is about $2.92 \times 10^{-4}$ at $n = 11$, about $1.30 \times 10^{-6}$ at $n = 21$, and below $10^{-10}$ by $n = 81$. For a 20 percent corruption level, a panel of $n = 31$ keeps the probability below $10^{-4}$ in the table.

The appendix also analyzes juror capacity. If challenges arrive at rate $\lambda$, panels require $n$ jurors, each case takes expected active time $h$, and each juror supplies active time $a$, then the minimum pool size is

$$
N_{\min} = \left\lceil \frac{\lambda n h}{a} \right\rceil.
$$

This turns scale into an operational planning problem: the needed juror pool grows with dispute volume and effort per case, and shrinks as available juror time increases.

The limits are equally important. The model must still address creator adoption, integration into existing platforms, subjective and evolving claims, expert domains, concentration of power, bias in community consensus, appeals, privacy, and <Highlight>the danger that an unchallenged claim is mistaken for a proven one</Highlight>.
