title: "Roles"
---

The framework separates the content-trust game into four roles. People may occupy more than one role in practice, but the model treats them separately to clarify incentives.

<div class="not-prose my-8 grid gap-3 md:grid-cols-2">
  <div class="rounded-lg border border-stone-300 bg-white p-4">
    <p class="text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-wide text-stone-500">Creators</p>
    <p class="mt-2 text-sm leading-relaxed text-stone-700">Publish content and may stake a veracity bond to signal confidence in factual claims.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="rounded-lg border border-stone-300 bg-white p-4">
    <p class="text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-wide text-stone-500">Challengers</p>
    <p class="mt-2 text-sm leading-relaxed text-stone-700">Contest a claim by submitting evidence and staking an equal counter-veracity bond.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="rounded-lg border border-stone-300 bg-white p-4">
    <p class="text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-wide text-stone-500">Jurors</p>
    <p class="mt-2 text-sm leading-relaxed text-stone-700">Review evidence, deliberate, vote, and provide written assessments that can affect reputation.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="rounded-lg border border-stone-300 bg-white p-4">
    <p class="text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-wide text-stone-500">Viewers</p>
    <p class="mt-2 text-sm leading-relaxed text-stone-700">Consume content and may help evaluate juror quality without joining the original dispute.</p>
  </div>
</div>

This role structure is meant to create accountability at every stage. Creators have "skin in the game" when making claims, challengers bear a cost for disputes, jurors are compensated for effort, and viewers help maintain the reputation layer that selects future jurors.
